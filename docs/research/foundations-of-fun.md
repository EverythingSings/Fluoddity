From Pong to Balatro: What Fifty Years of Great
Games Teach Us About the Foundations of Fun
TL;DR
Across five decades and wildly different forms — a two-paddle arcade machine, a
falling-blocks puzzle, a 350-million-selling sandbox, an $8-billion AR phenomenon,
and a one-person poker roguelike — the games that endure share a small set of
philosophical foundations, not technical ones: they are easy to learn and hard to
master, built on a tight core loop of action → feedback → reward, full of
meaningful choices, and tuned to keep players in a flow state between boredom
and anxiety.
Commercial scale and cultural longevity track these design fundamentals more
closely than budget or graphics: Minecraft (350M+ copies), Tetris (520M+ across
versions), GTA V (~225M units and ~$8.5B revenue), and single-developer hits like
Stardew Valley (41M+) and Balatro all obey the same principle Nolan Bushnell
articulated for Pong in 1971.
The deepest common denominator is that a great game is a machine for learning
— Raph Koster’s thesis that “fun is just another word for learning. ” Whether the
pattern being mastered is a block stack, a combat-timing window, a social bluff, or a
build order, great games deliver a steady stream of solvable problems with instant
feedback, escalating difficulty, and emotional or social stakes that make the mastery
matter.
Key Findings
1. “Easy to learn, hard to master” is the closest thing the medium has to a
universal law. Coined as Bushnell’s Law by Atari founder Nolan Bushnell around 1971
(“All the best games are easy to learn and difficult to master. They should reward the
first quarter and the hundredth”), Wikipedia it was born from the failure of his complex
first game, Computer Space — whose four equivalent-looking buttons, per game scholar
Ian Bogost, “had confused players… due to their lack of familiarity” — and the success
of the one-sentence-teachable Pong. Blizzard adopted it as a formal motto for Diablo,
Warcraft, and World of Warcraft; Wolfshead Online it explains Tetris, Flappy Bird,
Minecraft, League of Legends, and Elden Ring equally well.

2. Great games are “machines for learning. ” Designer Raph Koster’s A Theory of Fun
(2005) argues games are “exceptionally tasty patterns to eat up” and that “fun is just
another word for learning” — the brain releases endorphins/dopamine when it masters a
pattern. Crucially, Koster’s corollary defines both failure modes: a game gets boring
when the pattern is fully learned (mastery achieved), OR when it’s too trivial, OR too
hard to even perceive. The definition of a good game becomes “one that teaches
everything it has to offer before the player stops playing.” Theoryoffun
3. Flow is the target mental state, and it has a measurable shape. Psychologist
Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi’s flow theory — full immersion, energized focus, loss of time-
sense — occurs in a narrow channel where challenge matches skill. Too much challenge
→ anxiety; too little → boredom. Every well-tuned difficulty curve (Mario’s escalating
worlds, Celeste’s chapters, a roguelike’s scaling runs) is an engineering of this channel.
Flow’s preconditions — clear goals, immediate feedback, challenge-skill balance — are
themselves a design checklist.
4. A game is “a series of interesting decisions. ” Sid Meier’s famous aphorism
frames the other half of engagement: a decision is interesting only when no option is
obviously best, options aren’t equally attractive, and the player has enough information
to choose meaningfully. This is the engine of Civilization, but also of every loadout
choice in a shooter, every joker kept in Balatro, and every “fight or flee” in Dark Souls.
5. The core/compulsion loop is the atomic unit of engagement. The repeating cycle
— perform an action, get a reward, unlock a new possibility, repeat — is what keeps a
player in the chair. Its psychological roots run to B.F. Skinner’s operant conditioning and
variable-ratio reinforcement; its canonical game-design articulation is John Hopson’s
“Behavioral Game Design” (Gamasutra, 2001). The same loop underlies Candy Crush’s
match-three, WoW’s quest-reward-level cycle, and Pokémon’s catch-train-battle.
6. The most influential designers all preach “fun/idea first, technology later.”
Shigeru Miyamoto (gameplay before graphics or story; “the player is the hero, not the
designer”; the famous World 1-1 Goomba tutorial), Will Wright (“I’m giving the player a
toy, and the player is turning it into a game”; MasterClass “possibility spaces”), and Sid
Meier (“find the fun first and then use the technology to enhance the fun”) A-Z Quotes
converge on the same hierarchy of values.

The common denominators — synthesized
Stripping away genre, budget, and decade, the games above share these foundations:
1. Easy to learn, hard to master (Bushnell’s Law). A low barrier to entry plus a high
skill ceiling. Flappy Bird and Go both satisfy it; so do Tetris and Elden Ring.
2. Learning as the engine of fun (Koster). Every great game is a stream of solvable
patterns. When learning stops, fun stops — which is why endless variation
(procedural runs, new levels, PvP opponents, seasonal content) extends lifespan.
3. The tight core loop with immediate feedback. Action → feedback → reward →

new possibility. Instant, “juicy” feedback (Meier: always “acknowledge that the
player gave some input”) makes the loop satisfying. The compulsion-loop literature
(Hopson; Skinner) explains why variable rewards intensify it — and why this power
carries ethical weight.
4. Meaningful choice and player agency (Meier, Wright). Interesting decisions and
large “possibility spaces” make players feel ownership. Wright: players care most
about outcomes that are uniquely theirs.
5. Flow (Csikszentmihalyi). A difficulty curve that tracks rising skill, with clear goals
and feedback, keeps players in the immersive channel between boredom and
anxiety.
6. Emotional resonance and meaning. Miyamoto’s kyokan (shared feeling);
Undertale’s moral weight, Celeste’s empathy, Hades’s narrative-through-repetition,
BG3’s character-driven choice. The mechanics carry feeling.
7. Social and community dynamics. From the arcade crowd around a cabinet, to
WoW guilds, to Among Us lobbies, to League’s esports, multiplayer and community
convert a good loop into a lasting world. Among Us shows social context can be the
decisive multiplier.
8. Novelty balanced with familiarity. Bogost’s “catchiness” point: familiar enough to
grasp instantly, novel enough to intrigue. Franchises and roguelikes both live in this
tension.
9. Accessibility paired with depth. Pac-Man’s deliberate broadening, Celeste’s
assist modes, Mario’s simple-jump-with-hidden-depth. The best games let
beginners and experts find their level in one system.
10. Emergence over prescription. Breath of the Wild’s chemistry engine, Minecraft’s
blocks, The Sims’ systems — simple consistent rules that multiply into player-
authored stories Zelda Universe outlast hand-scripted, single-solution content.
What Flappy Bird shares with Zelda; what Tetris shares with WoW
Flappy Bird and Breath of the Wild look like opposites — one a one-tap punisher, the
other a 60-hour open world. But both are easy to learn, hard to master, both give instant
unambiguous feedback on every action, and both keep the player in flow by matching
challenge to growing skill. Tetris and World of Warcraft seem even further apart, yet
both run on the same loop: a never-ending stream of clearly-defined, immediately-
actionable tasks (clear a line / complete a quest), each delivering a small reward and a
slightly harder next problem — Zeigarnik’s “uncompleted task” pull operating at the
scale of seconds (Tetris) or weeks (WoW raid progression). The substrate differs; the

Recommendations
For anyone designing, evaluating, or studying games, the synthesis suggests a staged
checklist:
1. Start with the core loop, not the content. Before art, story, or technology,
prototype the 10-to-60-second cycle of action-feedback-reward and confirm it is
fun bare. Miyamoto spent months making Mario “fun just to move. ” Benchmark: if
the loop isn’t compelling with placeholder graphics, no polish will save it.
2. Pass the one-sentence test, then build a skill ceiling. Can you teach the game in
a sentence (Bushnell)? If not, simplify the entry. Then ask whether mastery can
deepen for hundreds of hours. Course-correction signal: if testers plateau in interest
quickly, the learning-pattern is exhausting too soon (Koster’s failure mode) — add
variation or depth.
3. Engineer the flow channel explicitly. Map the difficulty curve against expected
skill growth; use the “rising sawtooth” (ramp up, brief relief to feel heroic, ramp
again). Benchmark: rage-quits signal anxiety (too hard); disengaged drop-off signals
boredom (too easy).
4. Audit every choice for “interestingness” (Meier). Remove decisions where one
option dominates or all are equivalent; ensure players have the information to
choose. If a choice isn’t interesting, cut it. Game Developer
5. Decide where you sit on accessibility vs. depth — and serve both if possible.
Assist modes (Celeste), scalable difficulty (Mario), broadened themes (Pac-Man)
expand the audience without diluting mastery — increasingly a commercial as well as
ethical expectation.
6. Use compulsion-loop psychology responsibly. Variable rewards are powerful and
now legally scrutinized (loot boxes, freemium targeting). Recall ~96% of Candy
Crush players never pay, Kevuru Games so revenue concentrates on a vulnerable few
— treat retention mechanics as a duty of care, not just a KPI.
7. Plan for emergence and community. Where feasible, favor simple consistent
systems that multiply (chemistry engines, sandboxes) over single-solution content,
and design for sharing, watching, and playing together — the multipliers behind
Minecraft, Among Us, and League.
Caveats

Sales and player figures are noisy. “Best-selling ever” is genuinely disputed:
Minecraft (~350M as a single product) vs. Tetris (~520M only if all versions are
aggregated). Take-Two reports GTA V “sold-in” (shipped) figures, not sold-through.
Free-to-play “players/installs” (Fortnite, Pokémon GO) are not comparable to paid
“copies sold,” and revenue estimates from third parties (Sensor Tower, AppMagic,
SuperData) are modeled, not audited — note the Pokémon GO lifetime figure ranges
from ~$6B (Sensor Tower, 2022) to ~$8B (AppMagic, 2024).
The “$8.5 billion / most profitable entertainment product” claim for GTA V
traces to MarketWatch (2018, originally ~$6B) and AFP/Barron’s (late 2023, ~$8.5B);
rounder “$8 billion as of 2025” phrasing circulates mostly in lower-tier outlets
recycling those figures.
Quote attribution is imperfect. The Miyamoto line “a delayed game is eventually
good… ” is likely misattributed (per researcher Kate Willaert). Meier himself has said
“a series of interesting decisions” was a casual GDC definition, “not meant to be an
all-inclusive… definition. ”
These are principles, not formulas. They describe common denominators of
success after the fact; they do not guarantee it. Timing, distribution, marketing, and
luck (Among Us’s pandemic timing; streamer amplification) are real and sometimes
decisive. Some beloved or important games deliberately violate a principle (e.g.,
punishing difficulty over accessibility) in service of artistic intent.
“Fun” is not the only goal. Critics such as Ian Bogost note Koster’s fun-centric
model undervalues games as expression, art, or argument. Narrative and “walking-
sim” experiences can matter without optimizing the learning loop.
